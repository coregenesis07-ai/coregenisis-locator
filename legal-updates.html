First Step Act: consecutive-sentence “aggregation” issue

What is established: In Bonnie v. Dunbar, No. 24-6665, the Fourth Circuit affirmed the denial of habeas relief in November 2025. The majority treated consecutive sentences as a single aggregate term for the disputed First Step Act time-credit eligibility question. Judge Wynn dissented.

Federal law at 18 U.S.C. § 3632(d)(4) provides time credits for qualifying participation and lists convictions that make a prisoner ineligible to receive those credits. The Bonnie litigation challenges how that provision interacts with aggregated consecutive sentences.

Supreme Court status — last checked October 3, 2026: Bonnie v. Dunbar is docketed as No. 25-1363. The official docket shows the petition was distributed for the Court's September 28, 2026 conference. No later disposition is shown on the official docket as of this verification. A petition for review is not a Supreme Court ruling, so the challenged treatment should not be described as overturned.

What this means for families: This case may matter to people serving consecutive federal sentences where one conviction is listed as FSA-ineligible. Eligibility is individual and fact-specific. Preserve sentencing records, BOP credit calculations, and administrative-remedy records, and obtain qualified legal advice when a deadline or court filing may be involved.

FSA: sentencias consecutivas y “agregación”

Lo confirmado: En Bonnie v. Dunbar, el Cuarto Circuito confirmó la denegación del habeas corpus en noviembre de 2025. La mayoría trató las sentencias consecutivas como un término agregado para la cuestión disputada de elegibilidad de créditos FSA; el juez Wynn disintió.

La petición ante la Corte Suprema fue presentada como No. 25-1363 y el expediente oficial indica que fue distribuida para la conferencia del 28 de septiembre de 2026. Al momento de esta verificación no aparece una disposición posterior en el expediente. Una petición para revisión no significa que la Corte haya cambiado la ley o la política del BOP.

Current BOP FSA guidance: earning vs. applying credits

BOP's current First Step Act FAQ distinguishes between earning Federal Time Credits through qualifying assigned programming and applying earned credits toward prerelease custody or supervised release. Eligibility to earn, the amount earned, and whether credits can be applied are separate questions.

According to BOP's current guidance, eligible people may earn credits for successful participation in assigned evidence-based recidivism reduction programs or productive activities. Application of credits can depend on statutory requirements and recidivism-risk criteria; individual records and circumstances matter.

Useful official resources: BOP First Step Act FAQ · BOP FSA Overview · BOP Federal Time Credit Application Guide

Guía actual del BOP: ganar vs. aplicar créditos

La guía actual del BOP distingue entre ganar Créditos Federales de Tiempo mediante programas o actividades calificadas y aplicar esos créditos a custodia previa a la liberación o libertad supervisada. La elegibilidad para ganar créditos y la posibilidad de aplicarlos son preguntas distintas.

28 U.S.C. § 2255: challenging a federal conviction or sentence

Section 2255 generally permits a person in federal custody to ask the sentencing court to vacate, set aside, or correct a sentence on specified grounds, including constitutional or federal-law violations, lack of jurisdiction, a sentence above the lawful maximum, or another basis for collateral attack.

Deadline warning: § 2255 contains a one-year limitations period, but the statute provides four possible starting points. The correct deadline depends on the facts of the individual case. Second or successive motions also face additional statutory restrictions.

La sección 2255 permite, en determinadas circunstancias, que una persona bajo custodia federal solicite al tribunal que impuso la sentencia que la anule, deje sin efecto o corrija. Existe generalmente un período de limitación de un año, pero la ley establece cuatro posibles puntos de inicio. El plazo correcto depende de los hechos del caso.
